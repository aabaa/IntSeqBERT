\begin{frame}{Why Finding Good Problems Matters}
  \small
  \textbf{Terence Tao on mathematical problems}
  \smallskip

  I wrote recently about how the collection of \alert{\textbf{good, fruitful open problems}} is now being mined in a non-renewable fashion, leading to the potential scenario of \alert{\textbf{these problems becoming scarce}}.  This may seem unintuitive at first, since the set of possible problems one could ask is infinite.  Perhaps the following analogy can help: a country or region can suffer a \alert{\textbf{critical shortage of drinking water}} while simultaneously being \alert{\textbf{surrounded by a massive ocean}}.
  \par\smallskip
  One can easily generate any number of open problems in mathematics at will, such as working out the $10^{10^{10}}$th digit of pi.  But the vast majority of such problems are not worth focusing attention on: they show no particular propensity to \alert{\textbf{reveal any further insights or connections to other questions}}, or may either be too easy or too impossible relative to known techniques to learn anything from the exercise.  (1/4)
  \par\smallskip
  {\scriptsize Source: Terence Tao, Mathstodon, post 1/4 (quoted in full; emphasis added).\\
  \href{https://mathstodon.xyz/@tao/117237320796901560}{\texttt{mathstodon.xyz/@tao/117237320796901560}}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Long-Term Goal --- AI for Mathematical Discovery}
\small
  \begin{itemize}\tightlist
    \item \readout{New mathematical connections often begin with numerical patterns.}
      \begin{itemize}
        \item McKay noticed $196{,}884 = 196{,}883 + 1$; this led to \emph{Monstrous Moonshine}.
        \item The BSD conjecture and mirror symmetry also began with numerical patterns
              ($317{,}206{,}375$ rational curves in the mirror symmetry example).
      \end{itemize}
    \item AI now takes part in this kind of discovery. Examples include
          \textbf{AlphaProof}, the \textbf{Ramanujan Machine}, and \textbf{FunSearch}.
    \item \readout{Our long-term goal is to find similar arithmetic patterns across sequences
          and use them to suggest new conjectures.}
          The sequences may come from different fields of mathematics.
    \item We use the \textbf{OEIS}: 391{,}710 sequences, each with a precise definition.
          These definitions make mathematics accessible to computers.
    \item \readout{As a first step, we build a representation that captures arithmetic structure.}
          We then measure how much structure it learns.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why Multiplication Is Hard for Deep Learning}
\footnotesize
  \setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
  \readout{However, standard neural models struggle to learn multiplication rules.}\\
  With \textbf{spectral bias}, they learn low-frequency, addition-like patterns first (Rahaman+ 2019).
  \smallskip

  {\setlength{\tabcolsep}{3pt}\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.0}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.40\textwidth}p{0.56\textwidth}@{}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Model and task} & \textbf{What the result shows} \\
    \midrule
    \textbf{Threshold circuits}: $n$-bit multiplication\newline
    (Siu \& Roychowdhury 1994)
      & \alert{3 layers} are sufficient and minimal. A small circuit exists;
        training need not find it. \\
    \addlinespace[0.1em]
    \textbf{GPT-4}: decimal multiplication\newline
    (Dziri+ 2023)
      & Accuracy falls from \textbf{59\%} for $3{\times}3$-digit products
        to \alert{4\%} for $4{\times}4$-digit products. \\
    \addlinespace[0.1em]
    \textbf{2-layer Transformer}: multiplication mod $p$\newline
    (Power+ 2022)
      & It generalises only after a very long training period
        (\textbf{grokking}). \\
    \addlinespace[0.1em]
    \textbf{12-layer model}: implicit chain-of-thought\newline
    (Yao+ 2025)
      & It succeeds on \textbf{15-digit products} with a special training method. \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}}
  \smallskip

  These models and tasks differ, so their layer counts are not directly comparable.
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.1em}
    \item \readout{Tokenisation also hides arithmetic structure.}
          Token IDs do not show it directly; values outside the vocabulary become \texttt{UNK}.
    \item This matters for the OEIS: values range from single digits to $10^{210}$.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Key Idea --- Show Multiplicative Structure in the Input}
\small
  \begin{itemize}\tightlist
    \item \readout{So we give the model residues, which preserve addition and multiplication.}
          A residue is the remainder after division.
          Reduction mod $m$ is a \alert{ring homomorphism}:
          $\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}$.
    \item \readout{The residues stay small, even when the original values are very large.}
          For $10^{210}$, a residue is still in $\{0,\dots,m-1\}$.
          The sequence $(x_i \bmod m)$ keeps multiplicative structure.
    \item Classical number theory explains these \textbf{periodic patterns}:
      \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Fermat's little theorem} describes powers mod $p$
              (the period divides $p-1$).
        \item \textbf{Quadratic reciprocity} links residue information across primes.
        \item The \textbf{CRT} combines information from several prime powers.
      \end{itemize}
    \item \readout{Our hypothesis is that these inputs make multiplication easier to learn.}
          We call them the \emph{modulo spectrum}. They show multiplicative structure
          \alert{at depth zero}, before the network learns it across layers.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Task --- Masked Sequence Modelling on OEIS}
\small
  \begin{itemize}\tightlist
    \item \readout{To test this idea, we hide sequence terms and ask the model to predict them.}
          This is \alert{masked sequence modelling}: BERT-style learning for integers.
    \item We take up to the first \textbf{128 terms} and mask \textbf{15\%} of the positions.
          The remaining terms provide the context.
    \item \readout{This tests how well the model captures patterns in the sequence.}
          In \textbf{next-term prediction}, only the last term is masked.
    \item The \textbf{FACT} benchmark (NeurIPS 2022) tested a plain Transformer on five OEIS
          tasks. Unmasking was the hardest task. Our \textbf{Vanilla baseline} uses that architecture.
    \item \readout{For each hidden term, we predict its magnitude, sign, and residues.}
      \begin{enumerate}
        \item \textbf{Magnitude} measures log-scale size: $v_i = 1 + \log_{10}|x_i|$.
        \item \textbf{Sign} has three classes: $s_i \in \{+, -, 0\}$.
        \item \textbf{Residues} are $x_i \bmod m$ for every $m \in \{2, \dots, 101\}$.
      \end{enumerate}
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{IntSeqBERT --- Dual-Stream Architecture}
\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
\centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.58\textwidth]{fig1_architecture.png}
  \begin{itemize}\tightlist\footnotesize
    \item \readout{The model uses two input streams: magnitude and modulo.}
          The magnitude stream sends log-scale size and sign ($\mathbb{R}^4$) through an MLP.
    \item The modulo stream ($\mathbb{R}^{200}$) places residues on the \textbf{unit circle}:
          $\phi_m(r) = [\sin(2\pi r/m), \cos(2\pi r/m)]$.
          This uses sinusoidal positional encoding for values' residues;
          residues $m{-}1$ and $0$ stay neighbours.
    \item \readout{We combine the streams and pass the result to a Transformer.}
          With \textbf{FiLM}, the modulo stream scales and shifts the magnitude stream:
          $\mathbf{e}_i = (1 + \boldsymbol{\gamma}_i) \odot \mathbf{h}_i^{\mathrm{mag}} + \boldsymbol{\beta}_i$.
    \item We use a standard \textbf{pre-LN Transformer encoder}.
  \end{itemize}
  {\footnotesize\setlength{\tabcolsep}{8pt}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lcccr@{}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Model size} & Layers & Hidden dim. $d$ & Attention heads & Parameters \\
    \midrule
    Small  & 6  & 256 & 4  & 6.4M \\
    Middle & 8  & 512 & 8  & 29.0M \\
    Large  & 12 & 768 & 12 & 91.5M \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}\par}
  {\footnotesize Model sizes describe network capacity; value buckets describe $|x|$.\par}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Training --- Multi-Task Objective}
\small
  \begin{itemize}\tightlist
    \item \readout{We train the model to predict all three quantities together.}
      \begin{itemize}
        \item The \textbf{magnitude head} predicts size and its \alert{uncertainty},
              $(\mu_i, \sigma_i^2)$. The Solver uses both.
        \item The \textbf{sign head} predicts one of three classes.
        \item The \textbf{modulo head} has 100 independent classifiers.
              Each loss is normalised by the maximum entropy $\ln m$,
              so mod 2 and mod 101 contribute fairly.
      \end{itemize}
    \item We combine the losses with these weights:
          $\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{mag}} + \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sign}} + 2\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{mod}}$.
    \item We train on \textbf{219{,}765 OEIS sequences} for \textbf{200 epochs}.
          We use \textbf{FP32}, because values near $10^{210}$ overflow FP16.
    \item \readout{All experiments run on a single consumer GPU.}
          We use an RTX 3070\,Ti with 8\,GB, so other researchers can repeat the experiments.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Neural-Symbolic Decoding --- the CRT Solver}
\small
  \setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
  \begin{itemize}\small\tightlist
    \item \readout{First, the Solver generates candidates in the predicted magnitude range.}
          It searches the $3\sigma$ interval, using its size to choose a method:
      \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Dense} ($\Delta n \le 10^6$) checks every integer in the interval.
        \item \textbf{Sieve} ($\le 10^{14}$) uses CRT beam search with confident residues.
        \item \textbf{CRT} ($> 10^{14}$) generates large candidates directly with sparse CRT.
      \end{itemize}
    \item \readout{Next, it scores candidates using magnitude and all 100 modulo distributions.}
          For each $m$, it uses $P_m(n \bmod m)$.
  \end{itemize}
  \textbf{Probabilistic CRT:} \readout{We add weighted log-probabilities.}
  {
    \setlength{\abovedisplayskip}{3pt}
    \setlength{\belowdisplayskip}{3pt}
    \[
      S(n) = \underbrace{-\frac{(v_n-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}_{\text{magnitude log-likelihood}}
           + 0.3\underbrace{\sum_{m=2}^{101}\ln P_m(n\bmod m)}_{\text{all modulo log-probabilities}},
      \qquad v_n=1+\log_{10}n
    \]
    \footnotesize
    $P_m(r)$ is a residue probability. The weight 0.3 limits the effect of shared information.
    }
  \begin{itemize}\small\tightlist
    \item \readout{Finally, it returns the candidates with the highest scores.}
          It takes the top $k$ positive candidates and applies the sign; a zero sign returns 0.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Metrics --- and Three Questions}
  \footnotesize
  \setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
  \readout{We measure size, residues, and exact integer prediction.}\\[0.3em]
  {\setlength{\tabcolsep}{3pt}\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.1}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.23\textwidth}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.43\textwidth}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.30\textwidth}@{}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Metric} & \textbf{Definition} & \textbf{What it measures} \\
    \midrule
    \textbf{Magnitude Accuracy}\newline ($\mathrm{Acc}_{0.5}$)
      & Fraction within $0.5$ of the true log-scale magnitude
      & Approximate size; helps set the Solver's search range \\
    \addlinespace[0.2em]
    \textbf{MMA}\newline (mean modulo accuracy)
      & Mean classification accuracy over all 100 moduli
      & Accuracy of residue predictions \\
    \addlinespace[0.2em]
    \textbf{NIG}\newline (normalised information gain)
      & $1-\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}^{(m)}/\ln m$;\newline
        0 = no gain over uniform prediction; 1 = perfect prediction
      & Periodic structure learned for each modulus \\
    \addlinespace[0.2em]
    \textbf{Solver Top-$k$}
      & Fraction with the true integer among the top $k$ candidates
      & \alert{Exact integer prediction} \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}}
  \smallskip

  \textbf{For magnitude accuracy, an error within a factor of $\sqrt{10}\approx3.16$ is allowed.}\\
  True value $10^6$ $\to$ predictions in about
  $[3.2\times10^5,\ 3.2\times10^6]$ count as correct.
  \smallskip

  \readout{We ask three questions: does the dual stream help, where does it help,
  and can we predict exact integers?}\\
  Q1: overall gains. \quad Q2: gains by value bucket. \quad Q3: exact prediction.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{But What Did the Network Learn? The NIG Spectrum}
\centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{fig2_nig_spectrum.pdf}
  \begin{itemize}\tightlist\footnotesize
    \item \readout{We also ask what arithmetic structure the model has learned.}
          NIG measures this for each modulus from 2 to 101, beyond overall accuracy.
    \item \readout{IntSeqBERT learns more at every modulus, with a clear pattern of peaks.}
          It is above both baselines across the \textbf{entire NIG spectrum}.
    \item \readout{The highest peak is at modulus 96, followed by modulus 2.}
          This holds across all models and sizes: $96 = 2^5 \times 3$ (NIG\,$=0.629$).
          Mod 2 captures \textbf{parity}, which is common in the OEIS.
    \item Mod-2 accuracy is \textbf{85.7} for IntSeqBERT, 81.4 for Vanilla, and 72.1 for Ablation.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Finding --- Higher NIG with a Lower Totient Ratio}
\begin{columns}[T]

      \footnotesize
      \setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
      \textbf{Observation}\\
      \readout{We find higher NIG with a lower totient ratio.}\\
      Euler's ratio is $\varphi(m)/m = \prod_{p \mid m}(1 - 1/p)$.\\
      Pearson \alert{$r = -0.851$} ($p < 10^{-28}$).\\
      This is an experimental result, not a theorem.
      \medskip

      \textbf{Interpretation --- CRT aggregation}\\
      \readout{One modulus can combine information from several smaller moduli.}\\
      For example, $x \bmod 96$ gives $x \bmod 2,4,8,16,32$\newline
      and $x \bmod 3$ at the same time.
      \medskip

      \textbf{Next steps}\\
      \readout{This suggests testing more composite moduli.}
      \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.3em}
        \item Test \alert{highly composite numbers beyond 101} before adding more primes.
        \item Use these arithmetic patterns to \alert{compare sequences or datasets}.
      \end{itemize}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Backup --- Why Not Digit-Based Tokenisation?}
\begin{itemize}\tightlist
    \item \readout{Another option is to generate integers one decimal digit at a time.}
          Each digit is a token. We have not compared this approach experimentally in this paper.
    \item \readout{Our input gives the model residues directly.}
          We target the \alert{number-theoretic structure of the integer itself}.
          With digit tokens, the model must calculate residues from the digits.
    \item \readout{Digit-by-digit generation also changes the prediction task.}
          It predicts a sequence of digits, while our model predicts one integer value.
    \item We plan to compare the two settings under the same conditions.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Backup --- Limitations}
\begin{itemize}\tightlist
    \item \readout{Large integers remain difficult because residue predictions are uncertain.}
          In \textbf{13.4\%} of Solver calls, no valid candidate is found (\texttt{none} mode).
          Approximate CRT or less strict residue constraints may help.
    \item \readout{The dataset also affects our overall scores.}
          Many \texttt{nonn} sequences help sign accuracy;
          many slowly growing sequences help magnitude accuracy.
    \item \readout{Our experiments use limited compute and one random seed.}
          We use one \textbf{8\,GB GPU}, seed \textbf{42}, and a vocabulary of \textbf{20{,}003} tokens.
          Vanilla uses the \emph{FACT architecture} with our settings;
          we do not reproduce FACT's reported results.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Backup --- How Hard Is Multiplication for Neural Networks?}
\footnotesize
  \begin{itemize}\tightlist
    \item \readout{The answer depends on the circuit type and the training method.}
          In \textbf{circuit theory}, $n$-bit multiplication is not in AC$^0$
          (constant-depth AND/OR). Polynomial-size threshold circuits can do it with
          \alert{3 layers}, and 3 is optimal. Iterated products need only 4 layers
          (Siu \& Roychowdhury, \emph{SIAM J.\ Discrete Math.}, 1994).
    \item Log-precision \textbf{Transformers} are upper-bounded by uniform TC$^0$
          (Merrill \& Sabharwal 2023). This connects multiplication to model limits.
    \item \readout{Experiments show that learning multiplication can still be difficult.}
          GPT-4 zero-shot accuracy falls from \textbf{59\%} for $3{\times}3$-digit products
          to \alert{4\%} for $4{\times}4$-digit products
          (Dziri+, \emph{Faith and Fate}, NeurIPS 2023).
    \item A \textbf{2-layer Transformer} on modular arithmetic generalises only after a
          very long training period: \textbf{grokking} (Power+ 2022, arXiv:2201.02177).
    \item Standard training fails at multi-digit multiplication even at \textbf{90B} parameters.
          A \textbf{12-layer model} succeeds on \textbf{15-digit products} only with
          implicit-CoT training. Its attention forms a \alert{binary tree of partial products}
          (mechanistic analysis, arXiv:2510.00184, 2025).
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}
